I. The workshop

The workshop smelled of cedar shavings and lamp oil. Maps covered every wall — some finished and varnished, others half-drawn, coastlines trailing off into speculation. The apprentice's job, for the first year, was only to grind pigments and rule border lines, and to never, under any circumstances, touch the master's compass.

Naturally, the compass was all she thought about. It sat on the high shelf in a case of brass and bottle-green glass, and on certain evenings, when the light came through the west window at a particular angle, she was almost sure the needle moved on its own.

II. Concerning borders

A border, the master liked to say, is a fiction agreed upon by people who have never seen it. Rivers move. Mountains erode. The line holds because the map says it holds. This is why cartography is closer to law than to painting, and why a careless cartographer is more dangerous than a careless judge.

"Draw what is there. Where nothing is there, say so honestly — an empty quarter drawn empty is worth more than a beautiful invention."

She copied the maxim into her notebook, and then, because the margin looked bare, illustrated it with a small sea serpent. Some instincts cannot be trained away.

III. The commission

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, sealed with wax the colour of dried blood. A survey of the northern archipelago — forty islands, some charted only by rumour. Payment generous. Departure immediate. The master read it twice, folded it once, and looked at the high shelf for a long moment.

  • Six crates of instruments, packed in straw
  • Paper stock for two hundred sheets, oilcloth-wrapped
  • The good ink, and the cheap ink for drafts
  • The compass, case and all — handed to the apprentice to carry

She held it the way one holds a sleeping animal. The needle, she noticed, was not pointing north. It was pointing at her.
